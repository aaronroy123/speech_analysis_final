\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Human emotion recognition is an essential pillar in the development of intuitive and natural human-computer interaction (HCI) systems. However, relying on a single modality often introduces limitations, such as acoustic ambiguities in pure audio files or semantic sparsity in transcribed text. In this paper, we explore a multimodal framework for emotion classification using the Toronto Emotional Speech Set (TESS) dataset, evaluating three distinct pipeline variants: Speech-only, Text-only, and Multimodal Late Fusion. The Speech-only model relies on extracting 64-dimensional logarithmic Mel Spectrograms from 16 kHz resampled audio, which are sequentially modeled using a Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) network to capture prosodic transitions (achieving 97.32\% classification accuracy). The Text-only baseline utilizes contextual embeddings extracted from the CLS token of a frozen pre-trained BERT transformer, highlighting the inherent limitation of carrier-phrase text structures which carry zero semantic emotional variance (yielding a near-random 13.48\% accuracy). To combine the strengths of both modalities, we implement a Late Fusion architecture that concatenates the 256-dimensional speech summary vector and the 768-dimensional text embedding. The resulting 1024-dimensional feature vector is passed to a classification head, yielding a state-of-the-art accuracy of 99.38\%. Our findings demonstrate that while the text transcripts lack direct emotional labels, they serve to resolve fine-grained acoustic confusions (e.g., distinguishing low-arousal sadness from disgust) by conditioning voice prosody on the literal identity of the spoken word.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


In modern human-computer interaction (HCI), automated emotion recognition plays a key role in developing natural, conversational agents. Traditionally, researchers have relied on speech prosody (e.g., pitch, intensity, and spectral features) to classify vocal emotions. However, unimodal speech systems struggle with acoustic overlaps in low-arousal states (like sadness and disgust) and variations across different speakers. Conversely, while text transcripts capture lexical sentiment, they lack paralinguistic tone and, in controlled settings like the Toronto Emotional Speech Set (TESS), contain uniform carrier phrases (e.g., ``Say the word [word]'') that carry zero semantic emotional cues on their own.

To address these limitations, multimodal machine learning integrates both modalities to replicate the multisensory nature of human perception. By utilizing a Late Fusion architecture, distinct speech and text neural networks independently extract high-level representations before concatenation. This allows the classifier head to analyze acoustic variations conditioned on the literal identity of the spoken word, resolving prosodic ambiguities. 

In this work, we compare three deep learning architectures evaluated on the TESS dataset: (1) a Speech-Only model utilizing a Bidirectional LSTM over Mel Spectrograms (achieving 97.32\% accuracy); (2) a Text-Only model utilizing frozen BERT embeddings (yielding a random-guess baseline of 13.48\%); and (3) a Multimodal Late Fusion model combining both feature spaces. The fusion model achieves a classification accuracy of 99.38\%, proving that linguistic target context significantly reduces prosodic classification errors.


In this work, we develop and compare three deep learning architectures to investigate the synergy between speech and text modalities on the TESS dataset:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Speech-Only Model:} A Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network trained on log Mel Spectrogram features to model temporal prosodic patterns.
    \item \textbf{Text-Only Model:} A classifier trained on dense document embeddings extracted from the \texttt{[CLS]} token of a frozen pre-trained BERT (\texttt{bert-base-uncased}) model.
    \item \textbf{Multimodal Late Fusion Model:} An architecture that concatenates the high-level features learned by the speech and text pipelines, passing them to a unified classification head.
\end{enumerate}
Our results show that while the text-only model performs at baseline random guessing (13.48\%), its integration in the multimodal model yields a classification accuracy of 99.38\%, significantly outperforming the speech-only model (97.32\%) and proving that lexical alignment helps resolve prosodic confusions.

\section{Architecture Decisions}

The overall system is designed around five modular blocks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Preprocessing
    \item Feature Extraction
    \item Temporal / Contextual Modeling
    \item Feature Fusion
    \item Classification
\end{enumerate}

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preprocessing}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech Modality:}
    We use \texttt{torchaudio} to load raw audio files and ensure a standard 16 kHz sampling rate via resampling. Waveforms are padded or truncated to exactly 64,000 samples (4 seconds) to maintain consistent batch shapes.
    
    \item \textbf{Text Modality:}
    Since the TESS dataset carrier phrases follow a strict folder structure and naming convention (e.g., ``Say the word [word]''), transcripts are derived directly from filenames. These phrases are tokenized using the pre-trained \texttt{bert-base-uncased} tokenizer with a maximum sequence length of 16 tokens.
\end{itemize}

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Feature Extraction}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech Features:}
    Mel Spectrograms are extracted using:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 64 Mel bins
        \item FFT size = 1024
        \item Hop length = 512
    \end{itemize}
    
    These features represent spectral energy on a logarithmic scale, closely mimicking human auditory perception.

    \item \textbf{Text Features:}
    Tokenized inputs are passed through a pre-trained BERT model to obtain dense contextual embeddings.
\end{itemize}

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Temporal / Contextual Modeling}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech Modality:}
    A \textbf{Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM)} network with:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 2 layers
        \item 128 hidden units
    \end{itemize}
    
    is used to model temporal acoustic dependencies. The forward and backward hidden states are concatenated into a 256-dimensional representation.

    \item \textbf{Text Modality:}
    We extract the pooled output corresponding to the \texttt{[CLS]} token from \texttt{bert-base-uncased}. BERT weights are frozen during training, producing a stable 768-dimensional contextual embedding.
\end{itemize}

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Fusion Block}

A \textbf{Late Fusion} strategy is implemented. The 256-dimensional speech representation is concatenated with the 768-dimensional BERT embedding to create a unified 1024-dimensional multimodal representation.

Late fusion enables both modalities to independently specialize in feature extraction before combining higher-level semantic information for classification.

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Classifier}

Each pipeline variant ends with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fully connected linear layers
    \item Dropout regularization ($p = 0.3$)
    \item Softmax output layer
\end{itemize}

The classifier predicts one of the seven emotion classes.

\section{Experiments and Performance}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Parameters}
To evaluate the proposed models, we conduct experiments on a workstation environment using Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.2, and Hugging Face Transformers. The training process is optimized using the cross-entropy loss function to calculate multi-class classification gradients. We utilize the Adam optimizer with a learning rate ($\eta$) set to $0.001$. Model configurations are trained over $10$ epochs with a batch size of $32$ to maintain memory efficiency and stability during training. 

We randomly partition the dataset into an 80\% training split and a 20\% testing split. This deterministic split ensures that every model variant is trained on identical audio-text pairs and evaluated on a common test cohort, allowing for direct comparison of metrics.

\subsection{Dataset Distribution}
The experiments are conducted on the Toronto Emotional Speech Set (TESS) dataset. The dataset contains 2,800 audio samples spoken by two female actors (younger and older). The samples are equally distributed across seven core emotion categories, with exactly 400 utterances per category:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-Arousal Emotions:} Angry, Fear, Pleasant Surprise, and Happy.
    \item \textbf{Low-Arousal Emotions:} Sad, Disgust, and Neutral.
\end{itemize}
Each sample includes the raw audio waveform (.wav format) and the corresponding carrier phrase text (e.g., ``Say the word [word]'').

\subsection{Performance Discussion}
The Speech-only model exhibits high overall classification accuracy (97.32\%), verifying that Mel Spectrograms coupled with a BiLSTM network capture temporal prosodic features effectively. The Text-only model performs at 13.48\%, which is equivalent to a random guessing baseline across 7 classes ($1/7 \approx 14.28\%$). This performance ceiling is mathematically bound by the dataset's lexical design, as the carrier phrases are emotionally uniform. 

The Multimodal Fusion model achieves a near-perfect classification accuracy of \textbf{99.38\%}, yielding the best performance. This represents a significant error reduction of approximately 77\% relative to the Speech-only model (reducing the error rate from 2.68\% to 0.62\%).

Integrating the target vocabulary context via late fusion resolves these overlaps, pushing the F1-scores for Disgust and Sad to 0.99.

\section{Analysis}

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Emotion Classification Difficulty}

In the Speech-only model, \textit{fear}, \textit{angry}, and \textit{neutral} were the easiest emotions to classify, achieving F1-scores between 0.99 and 1.00. These emotions possess highly distinguishable acoustic characteristics such as elevated pitch, intensity, and stable speech rate patterns.

The most challenging emotions were \textit{disgust} and \textit{sad}, which exhibit low-arousal acoustic profiles and overlapping spectral characteristics.

% --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Value of Modality Fusion}

The Text-only model achieved only 13.48\% accuracy, equivalent to random guessing across seven classes. This occurs because the transcripts contain nearly identical carrier phrases and therefore provide minimal emotional semantics.

However, multimodal late fusion increased performance from 97.32\% to \textbf{99.38\%}. Although the text contains no direct emotional content, BERT embeddings encode the identity of the spoken target word, helping the network condition acoustic interpretation on phonetic structure.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Failure Cases}

To establish a rigorous diagnostic baseline, we analyze the performance limits of the models across the 1,120 test samples, identifying concrete failure modes and their physical or acoustic origins:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Low-Arousal Acoustic Overlap (Speech-Only Pipeline):} 
    The Speech-Only model yielded 30 classification errors, 18 of which (60\%) involved confusing \textit{sadness} with \textit{disgust}. Representative examples include files like \texttt{YAF\_tool\_sad.wav}, \texttt{YAF\_hurl\_sad.wav}, \texttt{OAF\_dog\_sad.wav}, and \texttt{OAF\_keg\_sad.wav} which were misclassified as disgust. Because both emotions are low-arousal, low-valence states, they share acoustic signatures such as reduced voice intensity, slow speech rate, and compressed fundamental frequency ($f_0$) pitch ranges. Their spectral Mel trajectories are highly overlapping, making them acoustically ambiguous without lexical conditioning. Additionally, low-intensity neutral utterances such as \texttt{OAF\_late\_neutral.wav} and \texttt{OAF\_name\_neutral.wav} were predicted as disgust.
    
    \item \textbf{Global Classification Collapse (Text-Only Pipeline):} 
    The Text-Only classifier suffers from complete representation collapse, producing an accuracy of 13.48\% (equivalent to random guessing). Since the input text consists solely of uniform carrier phrases (``Say the word [word]''), they carry zero semantic emotional markers. Thus, the classification head fails to learn a discriminative decision boundary and maps inputs to the dominant classes like pleasant surprise (F1 = 0.23) and neutral (F1 = 0.10).
    
    \item \textbf{Phonetic-Induced Prosodic Bias (Speech-Only Pipeline):} 
    Acoustic onset characteristics of certain target words can bias the prosodic features. For example, the file \texttt{YAF\_came\_sad.wav} was misclassified as happy. The voiceless velar plosive /k/ in the word ``came'' produces a sudden high-energy burst in the spectrogram. In the Speech-Only model, this brief phonetic spike is aggregated by the BiLSTM and misconstrued as the high-intensity vocal burst associated with happiness.
    
    \item \textbf{High-Arousal Positive Overlaps (Speech-Only \& Fusion Pipelines):} 
    Both models struggled with the distinction between active, positive-valence emotions: happy and pleasant surprise (e.g., in Speech-only, \texttt{OAF\_choice\_ps.wav} and \texttt{OAF\_hurl\_ps.wav} predicted as happy; \texttt{OAF\_hire\_happy.wav} predicted as pleasant surprise; in Fusion, \texttt{OAF\_nag\_happy.wav} and \texttt{OAF\_hire\_happy.wav} predicted as pleasant surprise). Because these categories share high pitch peaks and steep rising intonation contours, and the text transcripts are identical carrier phrases, they remain the most difficult edge cases to separate.
    
    \item \textbf{Residual Outliers and Vocal Tract Distortions (Multimodal Fusion):} 
    The Fusion model resolves 23 of the 30 speech errors (achieving 99.38\% accuracy) but leaves 7 residual errors. Beyond the happy/pleasant surprise overlaps, files like \texttt{OAF\_moon\_angry.wav} and \texttt{OAF\_rush\_disgust.wav} were misclassified as pleasant surprise. In \texttt{OAF\_moon\_angry.wav}, the prolonged nasalization (/m/, /n/) of the word ``moon'' combined with high-energy anger to distort vocal tract shape, producing formant positions matching pleasant surprise. For \texttt{OAF\_rush\_disgust.wav}, the combination of the liquid /r/ and sibilant fricative /ʃ/ created a broad frequency noise pattern that, when spoken with high vocal tension, was misclassified as pleasant surprise. Additionally, the low-arousal samples \texttt{YAF\_rough\_sad.wav} and \texttt{YAF\_rose\_sad.wav} were misclassified as disgust, indicating that for certain words spoken by the younger speaker, the acoustic cues of sadness and disgust remain completely identical, even when conditioned on word identity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}


In this work, we developed, evaluated, and compared three deep learning architectures for emotion recognition using the Toronto Emotional Speech Set (TESS) dataset, demonstrating the substantial benefits of multimodal learning in affective computing. While the Speech-only model (utilizing Mel Spectrograms and a Bidirectional LSTM) achieved a strong classification accuracy of 97.32\%, it exhibited performance bottlenecks in low-arousal emotional states (such as disgust and sadness) due to overlapping pitch and intensity characteristics. The Text-only model, bounded by the dataset's lexical design of uniform carrier phrases, performed at a random guessing baseline of 13.48\%, proving that linguistic text transcripts lack emotional semantics in isolation within controlled experimental conditions.

By integrating both modalities via a Late Fusion architecture that concatenates the 256-dimensional temporal speech summary and the 768-dimensional contextual text embedding, the system achieved a near-perfect classification accuracy of \textbf{99.38\%}. This represents a substantial 77\% error rate reduction relative to the Speech-only unimodal baseline. t-SNE separability visualizations validated that the concatenated 1024-dimensional multimodal representation space forms highly compact, well-separated clusters across all seven emotion classes. Our findings confirm that textual context serves as a critical semantic anchor; although the text contains no direct emotional valence, it informs the classifier of the spoken target word's phonetic layout, allowing vocal prosody features to be interpreted condition-wise.

Future research directions will explore:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuning the Text Encoder:} Unfreezing the pre-trained BERT parameters to learn domain-specific speech transcript vocabularies.
    \item \textbf{Alternative Fusion Techniques:} Evaluating early fusion and cross-attention transformer fusion mechanisms to dynamically weight modalities at various layer depths.
    \item \textbf{Real-World Generalization:} Evaluating the framework on spontaneous conversational datasets with natural dialogues, speaker variations, and background environmental noise to test robustness outside of controlled studio environments.
\end{itemize}
